When the Team Stops Talking to Each Other
NYT · Business · May 2026

When Marcus Chen took over as director of product at a mid-sized software company in Austin, he inherited a team that looked strong on paper: talented engineers, experienced designers, and a clear roadmap. What he did not inherit, however, was a functional communication structure.

"Everyone was doing their job," Chen said in a recent interview. "But no one was talking to anyone else. Deadlines were missed not because people weren't working, but because no one knew what the others were doing."

Chen's situation is not unusual. Across industries, managers are confronting a paradox: teams that are individually productive but collectively stalled. Researchers call it the coordination gap — the space between what individuals accomplish and what groups achieve together.

The Hidden Cost of Silence

A 2024 study by the Harvard Business Review found that teams with poor communication habits lost an average of 7.4 hours per week to duplicated work, misaligned priorities, and rework caused by insufficient handoffs. That translates to nearly one full workday per employee, per week.

The study surveyed 1,200 knowledge workers across six countries and found that the biggest barrier to effective team communication was not technology — nearly all teams had access to Slack, Zoom, or equivalent tools. The barrier was cultural: teams had developed norms of silence, where raising concerns or asking for help was perceived as a sign of weakness or incompetence.

"There's a workplace stigma around not knowing things," said Dr. Priya Kapoor, one of the study's lead researchers. "So people stay quiet, they make assumptions, and those assumptions compound into much larger problems down the line."

What Effective Managers Do Differently

The managers whose teams performed best shared several habits. They held short, structured daily check-ins — not to micromanage, but to surface blockers early. They explicitly rewarded team members who flagged problems before they escalated. And they modeled vulnerability themselves, openly discussing their own uncertainties in front of their reports.

"If I don't know the answer in a meeting, I say so," said Elena Vasquez, a senior engineering manager at a fintech firm in London. "I used to think that made me look weak. Now I think it makes the whole team more honest."

Accountability, the researchers found, was the most misunderstood lever. Managers often conflated accountability with punishment — holding people responsible after things went wrong. But the highest-performing teams defined accountability differently: as a shared commitment to transparency before things go wrong.

The Role of Workload and Morale

Excessive workload was cited as the single largest contributor to communication breakdown. When team members are stretched too thin, they default to heads-down execution, cutting corners on communication to protect their own output. Paradoxically, this siloed approach increases total workload across the team by creating the coordination failures described above.

Morale played a secondary but significant role. Teams with low morale communicated 40% less frequently than teams with high morale, even when their workloads were identical. This suggests that the relationship between communication and morale is bidirectional: poor communication damages morale, and low morale further reduces communication.

Chen eventually turned his team around by introducing what he calls a "no-blame postmortem" culture — a weekly meeting where the team reviews what did not go as planned, without assigning individual fault. Six months in, project delivery improved by 34%, and employee satisfaction scores rose significantly.

"The article I'd read on team management all talked about alignment and stakeholder buy-in," Chen said, laughing. "But the real fix was much simpler: I just got people to talk to each other again."
